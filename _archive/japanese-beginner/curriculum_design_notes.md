# Japanese Beginner Curriculum: Working Brief

## What I'm Trying To Do

I am building a beginner Japanese curriculum for absolute beginners who want to start talking right away.

The curriculum should be:

- pattern-based
- speaking-first
- practical from the beginning
- grammatically stacking
- usable in real lessons, not just theoretically well-organized

The goal is not to teach every piece of beginner grammar as fast as possible.
The goal is to help learners build speaking ability step by step without confusion or overload.

I want the curriculum to produce a learner who can:

- introduce themselves
- ask and answer simple everyday questions
- describe people, things, places, and routines
- shop, order, ask directions, and make simple plans
- talk about preferences, wants, past actions, reasons, and ongoing situations
- gradually move into more natural conversation

## What We Have To Keep In Mind

### 1. The curriculum must be communicative

Each lesson should lead to something the learner can actually say in real life.
Lesson goals should be framed around communicative outcomes, not just grammar labels.

### 2. The grammar must stack cleanly

Easier grammar should come first.
Later lessons should depend on forms learners already control.
We should avoid bottlenecks where an important structure appears too late.

Examples:

- plain form should come before lessons that depend on it, like quoting and relative clauses
- dense or confusing pairs should not be introduced together just because they look similar in English

### 3. A 15-25 minute lesson cannot carry too much

Two patterns per lesson only works when the patterns are very closely related.

A lesson should usually contain only one real load:

- one conjugation family
- one important contrast
- one dense vocabulary system

If a lesson includes a dense vocabulary system, the grammar frame should stay simple.

Examples of high-load content:

- position words
- dates and time expressions
- counters
- perspective-heavy giving/receiving language
- plain-form conversion

### 4. "Two patterns" does not always mean "easy"

Some pairings look neat on paper but are too heavy in class.

We should avoid:

- pairing unrelated patterns in one short lesson
- pairing look-alike structures that behave differently
- hiding a large vocab system inside a grammar lesson

### 5. The course should stay speaking-first

The learner should be able to use the new material quickly.
Even when the lesson teaches grammar, the real target is spoken output.

### 6. We should include beginner essentials that are easy to forget

The curriculum should not miss practical foundations such as:

- conversation repair language
- numbers, dates, and time ranges
- basic counters
- direction and position language
- family/politeness usage notes

These do not always need big standalone lessons, but they must be deliberately built into the course.

### 7. Not everything belongs in the beginner core

Some grammar may be useful but does not belong in the main beginner track if it creates too much cognitive load for too little speaking payoff.

The core should stay focused on high-frequency, high-utility beginner Japanese.
Heavier items can move into an upper-beginner bridge.

## Standard For A Good Lesson Goal

A strong lesson goal should be:

- easy to understand
- easy to teach in one short class
- useful in real conversation
- supported by earlier grammar
- broad enough to feel meaningful
- narrow enough to avoid overload

## Final Principle

This curriculum should feel like:

start talking immediately -> build confidence -> add control -> expand expression -> become more natural

It should not feel like:

memorize isolated grammar -> survive the lesson -> get overwhelmed next week

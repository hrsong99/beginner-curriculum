# Pronunciation & Listening — Table of Contents

**Goal:** make familiar English understandable on the first or second listen and make the learner’s own message easy to understand.

**18 lessons · 36 sound patterns.** Each lesson teaches two related sound/listening patterns inside useful phrases. The target is intelligibility, not erasing the learner’s accent. Productive control follows perception: hear the contrast → identify the meaning → repeat the chunk → use it in a short exchange.

> A “pattern” here is a repeatable sound-to-meaning relationship: a consonant contrast, stress rule, linking behavior, reduction, or intonation choice. Full and reduced forms are taught together.

---

## Unit 1 · Make the message clear · 6 lessons

**1. The important word — sentence focus**
- “I need the **BLUE** one.” — content words carry the beat
- “I need the blue one, not the **BLACK** one.” — contrastive stress corrects information

**2. Keep the ending — final consonants**
- `cap / cab`, `rice / rise` — final voicing can change meaning
- `work / worked`, `need / needs` — grammar endings must remain recoverable

**3. One or more — plural endings**
- `cups / bags / buses` — plural `-s` has /s/, /z/, and /ɪz/ patterns
- “two_cups” / “three_bags” — link the ending into the next word without deleting it

**4. Finished or repeated — past endings**
- `worked / played / wanted` — regular `-ed` has /t/, /d/, and /ɪd/ patterns
- “worked_all day” / “played_it” — link past endings into vowel beginnings

**5. Short vowels, different words I**
- `ship / sheep`, `live / leave` — /ɪ/ vs /iː/
- `full / fool`, `pull / pool` — /ʊ/ vs /uː/

**6. Short vowels, different words II**
- `bed / bad`, `men / man` — /e/ vs /æ/
- `cut / cot`, `luck / lock` — central vs open back vowel contrast (model variety noted)

*▸ Checkpoint 1: the listener identifies 8 minimal contrasts and transcribes the number/tense endings in six short phrases.*

## Unit 2 · High-value consonant contrasts · 5 lessons

**7. Light and right**
- `light / right`, `glass / grass` — /l/ vs /r/ at the beginning
- `feel / fear`, `collect / correct` — keep the contrast inside and at the end of words

**8. Fan, pan, van, ban**
- `fan / pan`, `coffee / copy` — /f/ vs /p/
- `van / ban`, `very / berry` — /v/ vs /b/

**9. Think and sink**
- `think / sink`, `three / see` — /θ/ vs /s/
- `then / zen`, `breathe / breeze` — /ð/ vs /z/

**10. Cheap, jeep, sheep**
- `cheap / jeep` — /tʃ/ vs /dʒ/
- `cheap / sheep`, `chair / share` — /tʃ/ vs /ʃ/

**11. Groups of consonants**
- `stop`, `street`, `spring` — keep initial clusters without adding a vowel
- `desk`, `asked`, `world` — simplify difficult endings only when meaning stays clear

*▸ Checkpoint 2: the learner resolves a name, number, and place through listen–choose and clarification tasks.*

## Unit 3 · Hear real connected English · 7 lessons

**12. Words have a strong center — word stress**
- `PHOtograph / phoTOGraphy` — related words can shift stress
- `REcord / reCORD` — stress can distinguish common noun–verb pairs

**13. Strong words, weak words — schwa**
- “a cup **of** tea” — articles, prepositions, and auxiliaries often weaken
- `can` /kən/ vs `can't` with a strong negative beat — listen for stress as well as consonants

**14. Contractions are ordinary English**
- `I am → I'm`, `she is → she's`, `we are → we're` — pronoun + be
- `do not → don't`, `I will → I'll`, `I have → I've` — auxiliary contractions

**15. Words connect**
- `an_apple`, `turn_it_on` — consonant-to-vowel linking
- `go_out`, `I_agree` — vowel-to-vowel connection with a smooth glide

**16. Sounds influence their neighbors**
- `did you → didja`, `would you → wouldja` — common coalescence for listening first
- `ten bikes`, `green park` — place assimilation may change a nasal without changing meaning

**17. Reductions without guessing**
- `going to → gonna`, `want to → wanna` — recognize casual reductions; keep full forms available
- `have to → hafta`, `got to → gotta` — reduction differs from careful or formal speech

**18. Intonation manages the conversation**
- rising vs falling yes/no questions — open confirmation vs settled check
- fall-rise in “I **like** it...” — signal reservation, incompleteness, or polite contrast

*▸ Final performance: listen to a short natural-speed exchange, recover its key details, request one clarification, then deliver a parallel exchange with clear focus and recoverable endings.*

## Delivery notes

- Model one consistent reference variety within a lesson, while exposing learners to more than one major variety over the track.
- Do not write casual reductions as if they were standard formal spelling.
- Score “message recovered by a listener” above “sounds native.”
- Recycle the current Core Pattern sentences so sound work never becomes a disconnected list of words.
